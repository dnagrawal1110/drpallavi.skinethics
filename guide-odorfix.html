Sweat Has No Smell — The Bacteria Do

This is the most important thing to understand about body odour: sweat itself is odourless. Fresh perspiration from the eccrine glands is mostly water, salt, and trace minerals. The smell comes from what happens after sweat reaches the skin surface.

The Odour Production Pathway
💧
Apocrine Gland Secretion
Apocrine glands (found in the underarm, groin, and breast area) secrete a protein and lipid-rich fluid. Unlike eccrine sweat, this secretion contains compounds that bacteria can metabolise.
🦠
Corynebacterium Colonisation
Corynebacterium — a genus of bacteria that thrives in warm, moist, protein-rich environments — is the primary organism responsible for underarm odour. It colonises the skin and the sweat gland ducts.
⚗️
Lipid Breakdown → Volatile Fatty Acids
Corynebacterium secretes enzymes that break down the lipids in apocrine secretion into short-chain volatile fatty acids — most notably 3-methyl-2-hexenoic acid. These molecules evaporate rapidly at body temperature and are responsible for the characteristic odour.
🌡️
Monsoon Amplification
Higher ambient temperature and humidity both accelerate bacterial growth and increase sweat production. More substrate + faster bacterial activity = stronger odour in monsoon.

"Deodorant works by masking the smell or reducing sweat. It does not address the bacterial colony producing the odour — which is why it works for hours, not days."

Two Separate Problems at the Underarm

01
Body Odour
Cause: Bacteria
Corynebacterium breaking down apocrine secretion into volatile fatty acids. A microbiology problem — needs antibacterial treatment targeting the bacterial load.
02
Dark Underarms
Cause: Friction + PIH
Chronic friction from clothing and shaving triggers post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation in the thin underarm skin. A pigmentation problem — needs melanin-targeting treatment.

Most people try to treat both with the same product — typically a whitening deodorant. Neither problem is solved effectively because each requires a mechanism-specific approach.

Why Underarm Skin Darkens

The Friction — Pigmentation Pathway
Repeated friction from clothing, tight sleeves, and rubbing during movement creates micro-trauma in the underarm skin.
Shaving causes the same — each pass of the razor removes not just hair but the surface skin layer, triggering inflammation at a microscopic level.
Inflammation activates melanocytes in the basal layer of the thin underarm skin. Indian skin (Fitzpatrick III–V) is particularly prone to PIH — any inflammation triggers pigment overproduction.
Melanin accumulates in the epidermis. The skin appears darker. Without treatment, each new shave or friction event reinforces the pigment deposit.
Deodorant alcohol and fragrance also contribute — they cause low-grade chemical irritation on already-sensitive skin, adding a third friction source.

Clinical Treatments — One for Each Problem

O1
Benzoyl Peroxide Wash + Clindamycin
For Body Odour — Targets Corynebacterium
Benzoyl Peroxide is a broad-spectrum antibacterial that penetrates the follicle and reduces Corynebacterium load at the source — not just on the surface. Used as a wash (2.5–5%) applied and rinsed during showering. Clindamycin topical solution is prescribed for more persistent bacterial overgrowth. Together, they reduce the bacterial population responsible for odour production significantly — lasting effect rather than hourly masking.
O2
Mandelic-Arbutin Peels
For Dark Underarms — Clears Melanin Deposits
Mandelic acid exfoliates the thickened, pigmented surface layer of underarm skin while being gentle enough for the sensitive axillary (underarm) region. Arbutin (alpha-arbutin) inhibits tyrosinase, the enzyme that converts tyrosine into melanin — preventing new pigment formation while Mandelic acid clears existing deposits. A series of 4–6 sessions spaced 3–4 weeks apart produces significant and lasting lightening without the bleaching side effects of older treatments like hydroquinone.
O3
Laser Hair Removal
Removes the Source of Friction
Permanent hair removal eliminates shaving — which is one of the primary friction triggers for PIH. Laser hair removal also reduces the apocrine follicle density in the treated area, which has the secondary benefit of reducing the substrate available for Corynebacterium to colonise. Most patients notice both improved pigmentation and mild reduction in odour following a full laser hair removal course.

Common Myths

Whitening deodorants can fix dark underarms.
Whitening deodorants contain low concentrations of kojic acid or niacinamide — far below the levels needed for clinical pigmentation treatment. They may maintain brightness but cannot significantly clear established PIH. They also continue the friction and chemical irritation that caused the darkening in the first place.
Body odour is a hygiene problem — more washing is the solution.
Frequent washing removes sweat but does not eliminate the Corynebacterium colony embedded in the skin and follicles. The bacteria repopulate within hours. Targeting the bacterial load with appropriate antibacterial agents is more effective than washing frequency.
Waxing is better than shaving for underarm pigmentation.
Waxing causes more acute trauma than shaving — it tears hair from the follicle, triggering a stronger inflammatory response and therefore more PIH. Laser hair removal is the correct long-term solution for both hair removal and pigmentation when darkening is the primary concern.
Natural remedies like lemon juice and baking soda lighten underarms safely.
Lemon juice (pH ~2) and baking soda (highly alkaline) both disrupt the skin barrier — causing inflammation that worsens PIH. Lemon juice also causes phototoxic reactions in sun-exposed skin, darkening rather than lightening. These are among the worst things to apply to already-inflamed underarm skin.
