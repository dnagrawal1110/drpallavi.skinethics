Why Does Hair Fall Out?

Your hair follows a precise biological clock. When that clock is disrupted, the shedding begins — and it's almost never about your shampoo.

Think of Each Strand as a Leaf on a Tree

A tree doesn't lose all its leaves at once — and neither does your scalp. In autumn, old leaves fall to make way for new growth. That's biology, not damage.

Your hair works exactly the same way. Every strand has a lifespan. It grows, it rests, it falls. A new hair replaces it. The system is designed to shed.

"The problem isn't that hair falls. The problem is when the cycle breaks down and new hair stops replacing what was lost."

Understanding the cycle is the difference between panicking over your drain every morning — and knowing when to actually seek help.

The 4 Phases of the Hair Growth Cycle

At any given moment, each strand on your head is in a different phase — all running on separate timers simultaneously.

Phase 01
Anagen Active Growth
85%
The follicle is actively producing hair. Root cells divide rapidly, pushing the strand longer. The only phase where real growth occurs.
2 – 7 years
Phase 02
Catagen Transition
2%
The follicle shrinks, detaches from its blood supply, and prepares to shed. Growth stops completely here.
2 – 3 weeks
Phase 03
Telogen Resting
13%
The follicle rests. The old hair holds loosely at the scalp while a new strand quietly begins forming beneath it.
3 – 4 months
Phase 04
Exogen Shedding
—
The old strand sheds as the new hair pushes through. The hair you see on your brush. A sign the cycle is working correctly.
Days to weeks

What Is Actually Normal?

Before counting every hair on your pillow, here's the number every dermatologist will cite first:

50
strands / day
—
100
strands / day

With roughly 100,000 hairs on your scalp, losing 50–100 per day is less than 0.1%. This is entirely normal Exogen shedding — the old making way for the new.

What matters more than the number is the trend. A sudden increase, a visibly thinning crown, or a wider part line — those are the signals worth acting on.

4

What Breaks the Cycle

When too many follicles enter the resting phase at once, or the growth phase shortens significantly, visible thinning follows. These are the six most common triggers:

01

Nutritional Deficiency

Iron, ferritin, Vitamin D, B12, and zinc are the most common culprits in Indian patients. The hair follicle is one of the body's most metabolically active structures — it needs consistent fuel. Crash diets and restrictive eating are classic triggers.

02

Hormonal Imbalance

DHT (Dihydrotestosterone) is the primary driver of androgenetic alopecia — the most common type of hair loss in both men and women. Thyroid disorders and PCOS are also frequent underlying causes that are frequently diagnosed late.

03

Telogen Effluvium

A sudden body shock — illness, surgery, childbirth, severe stress, rapid weight loss — pushes large numbers of follicles into the resting phase at once. The shedding appears 2–4 months later, which is why patients often cannot trace it back to the original cause.

04

Scalp Inflammation

Chronic dandruff, seborrheic dermatitis, psoriasis, or a blocked scalp environment creates persistent inflammation around the follicle. That inflammation shortens the Anagen phase — producing progressively finer, weaker strands until they stop growing altogether.

05

Mechanical Damage

Tight hairstyles (traction alopecia), chemical treatments, excessive heat, and aggressive brushing cause physical trauma to both the shaft and the follicle. Sustained traction can permanently scar the follicle over time.

06

Medications & Autoimmune Conditions

Blood thinners, certain antidepressants, and chemotherapy are known to disrupt the cycle. Alopecia Areata — where the immune system attacks follicles — produces patchy loss and requires specialist management.

Myths Worth Unlearning

Daily oiling promotes hair growth.
Hair growth occurs deep inside the follicle — oil cannot penetrate to that depth. Heavy oiling can block follicle openings and worsen scalp conditions. Scalp massage (not the oil itself) is what improves blood circulation.
Changing your shampoo will stop hair fall.
Shampoo cleans the scalp surface. Hair loss is driven by internal factors — hormones, nutrition, the growth cycle. No shampoo can reverse a disrupted biological cycle.
Cutting hair makes it grow back thicker.
Hair thickness is determined entirely by the follicle — not the blade. Cutting removes the tapered tip of the strand, making the cut end look blunter. The root is completely unaffected.
Biotin supplements cure hair loss.
Biotin helps only in cases of genuine biotin deficiency — which is rare. Most people who supplement already have adequate levels. The root cause of hair loss is almost never biotin.
7

When to See a Dermatologist

A proper hair loss evaluation looks beyond what's falling — it examines the follicle, the scalp environment, and your bloodwork together. Book a consultation if you notice:

  • Hair loss persisting beyond 3 months without a clear trigger
  • Visible thinning at the crown, temples, or along the part line
  • Sudden patchy loss (could indicate Alopecia Areata)
  • Scalp itching, burning, or flaking alongside hair fall
  • Family history of baldness with early signs of recession
  • Significant hair fall following illness, surgery, or extreme stress

"Early intervention consistently yields better results. Waiting six months to 'see if it settles' usually means treating a more advanced stage."
