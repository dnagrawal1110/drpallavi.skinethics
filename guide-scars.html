Your Skin Is Like a Road — and Acne Leaves Potholes

Imagine a road with potholes. You can repaint it, resurface the top layer — but if you don't fill the holes first, the surface will never be truly smooth. Every layer of tarmac you add just follows the dip beneath it.

Each type of pothole needs a different repair method

Acne scars work exactly the same way. The inflammation from acne damages the collagen structure beneath the skin's surface. The visible depression or raised tissue is what remains after the infection resolves — and different types of damage require completely different repair strategies.

"Applying brightening creams on an atrophic scar is like painting over a pothole. It doesn't fix the structural damage — it just makes it harder to see temporarily."

The 4 Types of Acne Scars

Identifying your scar type is the single most important step — because the treatment that works brilliantly for one type can be ineffective or even harmful for another.

Type 01
Ice Pick Scars
Deep, narrow channels that extend down into the dermis. They look like small puncture marks and are difficult to treat because of their depth. Caused by severe cystic acne where the infection tracks deep into tissue.
Best Response To
CROSS technique (Chemical Reconstruction of Skin Scars), TCA focal peels, Fractional CO₂ laser — not standard resurfacing
Type 02
Boxcar Scars
Wide, shallow to medium-depth depressions with sharply defined vertical edges. They give skin a pitted, uneven appearance and are most visible at an angle in natural light. The most common type on the cheeks and jaw.
Best Response To
Microneedling, Fractional laser resurfacing, subcision, filler for deeper variants
Type 03
Rolling Scars
Broad, shallow depressions with sloping edges that give skin a wave-like, undulating texture. Caused by fibrous bands tethering the skin surface down to deeper tissue. More visible in certain lighting and tend to look worse as skin ages and loses elasticity.
Best Response To
Subcision (releasing the fibrous bands) followed by microneedling or PRP — the combination matters here
Type 04
Hypertrophic & Keloid Scars
Raised scars where the body overproduces collagen during healing. Hypertrophic scars stay within the original wound boundary; keloids extend beyond it. More common in darker skin types and require a fundamentally different treatment approach — stimulating collagen here makes things worse, not better.
Best Response To
Intralesional corticosteroid injections, silicone sheets, Nd:YAG laser — never aggressive resurfacing

What Actually Happens to Your Skin After Acne

Understanding the collagen timeline helps you understand why scar treatment takes months, not weeks:

Days 1 – 10

The Inflammatory Phase

Acne infection triggers inflammatory cytokines that destroy local collagen fibres. The immune response clears the bacteria — but also damages the surrounding dermal architecture in the process.

Weeks 2 – 6

Proliferative Phase

Fibroblasts rush to the area and begin producing new collagen to repair the damage. This collagen is disorganised — laid down rapidly, not in the structured parallel pattern of healthy skin.

Months 3 – 12

Remodelling Phase

The body slowly reorganises that disorganised collagen. This is the longest phase — and the window where targeted treatments are most effective at influencing the final result.

After 12 Months

The Scar Matures

The scar reaches its final form. Atrophic scars represent a collagen deficit; hypertrophic scars represent a surplus. Both are now structural — not something topicals can reverse without intervention.

The Treatment Ladder

Scar treatment follows a logical progression — starting with the least invasive options and escalating based on scar type, depth, and response:

1

Targeted Topicals

Tretinoin, niacinamide, azelaic acid, and tranexamic acid. Effective for post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (the flat dark marks left by acne) — but they cannot structurally correct atrophic or raised scars.

2

Chemical Peels

Glycolic, TCA, or salicylic acid peels improve skin texture, reduce discolouration, and prepare the skin for deeper treatments. A good first clinical step. Results are gradual and cumulative — not dramatic after a single session.

3

Microneedling ± PRP

Creates controlled micro-injuries that trigger the body's own collagen cascade. Effective for boxcar and rolling scars. Adding PRP (Platelet-Rich Plasma) enhances and accelerates the collagen response. Requires 4–6 sessions spaced 4 weeks apart for meaningful results.

4

Subcision

A needle is inserted beneath rolling scars to physically cut the fibrous bands tethering the skin downward. Once released, the skin lifts — and subsequent microneedling or filler is far more effective. A critical step often skipped by clinics unfamiliar with scar anatomy.

5

Fractional Laser Resurfacing

Fractional CO₂ or Erbium lasers create thousands of microscopic columns of controlled injury across the scar surface, stimulating deep collagen remodelling. The most effective single modality for moderate to severe atrophic scarring. Requires careful skin type assessment on Indian skin to prevent post-treatment pigmentation.

6

Dermal Fillers

Hyaluronic acid or collagen-stimulating fillers injected directly beneath deep scars to physically lift them level with the surrounding skin. Results are immediate but temporary (12–18 months). Best used as an adjunct to other treatments, not as a standalone solution.

Realistic Expectations by Scar Type

Scar Type Achievable Improvement Timeline
PIH (Dark Marks) 70–90% clearance 3–6 months with topicals + peels
Boxcar 50–70% improvement 6–9 months, 4–6 sessions
Rolling 60–80% improvement 6–12 months with subcision combo
Ice Pick 30–50% improvement Slowest — 9–18 months, multiple modalities
Hypertrophic Significant flattening 3–6 months with injections

"There is no single treatment that erases all acne scars. The goal is meaningful, visible improvement — and a combination protocol almost always outperforms a single modality."

A Note on Indian Skin

Important for Fitzpatrick Types III – V

Indian skin has a higher risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) following any treatment that causes inflammation — including laser, microneedling, and chemical peels. This does not mean these treatments cannot be done. It means they must be done at the right settings, with the right pre-treatment protocol, and by someone with experience treating darker skin tones.

A proper pre-treatment course of topicals (tyrosinase inhibitors, sun protection) for 4–6 weeks before any procedure significantly reduces PIH risk on darker skin. Never skip this step.
